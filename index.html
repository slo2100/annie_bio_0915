Chemical evolution → organic evolution

Origin of life

Two eras, back to back: chemical evolution (non-living chemistry slowly building life-like structures) and organic evolution (living things evolving by natural selection, once FLO and LUCA existed).

An idea that was ruled out first

Spontaneous generation / vitalism — the old belief that life springs from non-living matter through a mysterious "life force" (vitalism). Louis Pasteur disproved it with his swan-necked flask experiment: broth boiled in a flask with an open, curved neck stayed sterile indefinitely, even though it was still exposed to air — because the curve trapped microbes before they could reach the broth. Break the neck (or tip the flask so broth touches the trapped debris) and decay starts immediately. Conclusion: biogenesis — life only comes from existing life. That still leaves the real question open: where did the very first living thing come from?

Where did the organic molecules come from? Three hypotheses

These aren't competing answers — evidence suggests all three could have contributed.

Primordial soup

Miller–Urey experiment (1953)

Simulated early Earth's reducing atmosphere (no free O₂: H₂, CH₄, NH₃, water vapor) with electric sparks standing in for lightning. Result: simple organics like formaldehyde and hydrogen cyanide, which can polymerize into amino acids and nucleic-acid bases. Repeating the experiment with other early-atmosphere gas mixtures gives similar results.

Extraterrestrial delivery

Meteorites & asteroids

The 1969 Murchison meteorite carried amino acids, lipids, sugars, and uracil. NASA's Bennu asteroid sample (analyzed 2024–25) contained over 16,000 kinds of organic molecules, including amino acids and all four DNA/RNA nucleobases. Space may have rained down life's raw ingredients.

Hydrothermal vents

"Most likely scenario," per your notes

At mid-ocean tectonic-plate boundaries, cold seawater seeps into cracks, is warmed and mineral-enriched by the mantle, and rises back up — concentrating chemicals exactly where catalytic minerals are sitting.

Hydrothermal vents up close

Black smokers

Extremely hot (≈350–700°C) and acidic, rich in iron, nickel, and sulfide minerals. Larger pH and temperature gradients; more biodiversity clusters around them today.

White smokers

Cooler (≈100–150°C) and more alkaline. Walls made of calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) with Fe/Ni/Cu ions as possible catalysts. Riddled with microscopic pores — sheltered, semi-permeable pockets where proton gradients could build up and organic molecules could concentrate.

Both types share the ingredients biologists think life needed: a source of chemical energy, mineral catalysts, and a way to isolate and concentrate molecules — the same jobs a cell membrane does today.

The shared mechanism: chemiosmosis

Peter Mitchell's proton-motive-force hypothesis (Nobel Prize, 1978): protons (H⁺) build up on one side of a membrane, creating stored potential energy. Letting them flow back through a channel — ATP synthase — drives ATP production. Every mitochondrion still runs on this today, and hydrothermal-vent micropores may have run on it before any cell existed.

Protobionts: "before life," made in the lab

Coacervates

Droplets of protein + carbohydrate that cluster together through hydrophobic interactions. In lab experiments they could even carry out a simple enzyme reaction (turning starch into maltose).

Microspheres

Made from proteinoids — artificially created proteins — forming tiny (~2 μm) spherical structures.

Liposomes

Made of amphipathic fatty acids: a polar, hydrophilic carboxyl head and a nonpolar, hydrophobic methyl tail. These spontaneously arrange into a bilayer membrane — isolating and protecting an interior, concentrating molecules, and preventing pH swings that would stop reactions.

None of these are alive — they have some characteristics of life (a boundary, some chemistry inside) but not all of them (no reliable self-replication, no heritable code).

The RNA World hypothesis

NASA defines life as "a self-sustained chemical system capable of undergoing Darwinian evolution." Break that down, and RNA fits surprisingly well:

i

Chemical system

Controlled by catalytic molecules that carry out metabolism. RNA can act as a ribozyme — an RNA enzyme (the ribosome itself is one) — catalyzing reactions and helping build macromolecules.

ii

Self-sustained

The same molecules ("code") must be copied and passed on each generation. RNA can self-replicate — lab experiments show short RNA fragments forming spontaneously and then copying themselves.

iii

Darwinian evolution

Molecules must be able to mutate — and a more efficient mutated form should become more common over time. In vitro experiments (10¹⁵ RNA variants, repeated selection + amplification cycles) show exactly this: the most efficient catalytic RNA sequences came to dominate the population — chemical selection.

So why did life switch to DNA? DNA is a more stable, double-stranded molecule. Modern eukaryotic cells still carry the enzyme reverse transcriptase, which converts RNA into DNA — and molecules like ATP, NAD, FAD, and coenzyme A are all RNA-based, thought to be molecular "relics" of an earlier RNA world.

FLO → LUCA

FLO

First Living Organism

Possibly RNA-based (a ribozyme that could replicate and evolve). Had an energy molecule and a membrane. Might have been a chemoautotroph — living at a hydrothermal vent, oxidizing H₂S to build a proton gradient (PMF) and make ATP via chemosynthesis, the same basic strategy chemosynthetic bacteria use today.

LUCA

Last Universal Common Ancestor

DNA-based. Had a phospholipid bilayer membrane, used ATP, and stored its genome in DNA — the same three traits every living cell on Earth still shares. LUCA's descendants split into bacteria, archaea (both prokaryotes), and eukaryotes.

Real-world evidence: chemosynthesis at vents today

In 1977, the submersible Alvin discovered thriving ecosystems at deep-sea hydrothermal vents — including giant tube worms (Riftia pachyptila). Tube worms have no mouth, gut, or anus. Instead, an internal organ called the trophosome houses trillions of chemosynthetic sulfur bacteria. The worm's blood (using hemoglobin) ferries H₂S, O₂, and CO₂ to the bacteria, which oxidize H₂S for energy and fix CO₂ into organic molecules the worm can use. It's a living example of chemosynthesis supporting an entire food web — and a working model for how FLO might have made a living.

From pollen to seed

Pollen lands on the stigma → special proteins let compatible pollen germinate and grow a pollen tube down the style → the tube reaches the ovary and delivers sperm (n) to the egg (n) inside the ovule → fertilized egg / zygote (2n) → mitosis (2n → 2n) → growth and development → seed.

Meiosis: the reduction division

Meiosis is a reduction division that produces cells with half the normal number of chromosomes. Human example: a body cell has 2 × 23 = 46 total chromosomes (23 kinds, 2 copies of each — one paternal set, one maternal set). Meiosis produces sperm or egg cells with just 23 chromosomes — one of each kind.

Hermaphroditic flowers

Most flowers have both reproductive organs (stamen and pistil) in the same structure. That raises two questions worth knowing for the test: can a flower fertilize itself, and if so, what structural or timing adaptations do flowers use to control whether self-pollination or cross-pollination happens?

Prokaryotic & eukaryotic cells

Cell structure

Word roots do a lot of work here: pro = before, karyon = nucleus, eu = true. A prokaryote exists "before a nucleus"; a eukaryote has a "true" nucleus.

Think of it like an efficiency apartment versus a mansion. A prokaryote is a one-room apartment — everything happens in the same open space. A eukaryote is a mansion — membrane-bound organelles give each job (energy, digestion, packaging) its own room, so processes don't interfere or compete with each other.

Prokaryotic cell (bacteria)

  • Three shapes: coccus (round), bacillus (rod, e.g. E. coli), spirillum (spiral)
  • Free-floating DNA — not enclosed in a membrane
  • Cell wall, cell membrane, cytoplasm
  • Small dots = ribosomes (protein synthesis)
  • Some have a flagellum for propulsion — those without one are non-motile

Animal cell (eukaryote)

  • Membrane-bound nucleus (DNA + nucleolus)
  • Mitochondria, ER, Golgi, lysosomes, vesicles
  • No cell wall, no chloroplast
  • Small or temporary vacuoles

Plant cell (eukaryote)

  • Everything an animal cell has, plus:
  • Cell wall (made of cellulose)
  • Chloroplasts (photosynthesis)
  • One large, permanent central vacuole

Root hair cell

Many thin projections increase surface area for absorbing water and nutrients from soil.

Muscle cell

Bundled into smaller contractile units (myofibrils), allowing the cell to contract.

Nerve cell

Many branching connection points let it pass messages to lots of other cells.

Sperm cell

Small mass and a tail for transport; surface proteins help it identify (and be identified by) the right egg.

Natural selection & convergent evolution

Evolution

Evolution is a change in a population's traits over time. It happens at the population level — never to a single organism during its own lifetime.

How natural selection works

Selection acts on traits that are controlled by genes — traits that can be passed on. When conditions change, individuals whose traits happen to work better in the new conditions survive and reproduce more (differential reproduction). Their traits become more common in the next generation.

Change is also restricted: in the short term by weather, and in the long term by whether the environment can support the population at all — if conditions get too extreme, extinction is possible.

Where variation comes from

Mutation — a random change in DNA — is the ultimate source of genetic variation. A mutation that happens during ordinary cell division isn't passed to offspring. Only a mutation in a sex cell (sperm or egg) can be inherited and matter for evolution.

Convergent evolution: carnivorous plants

Convergent evolution: unrelated species independently evolve similar traits because they face similar environmental pressures — not because they share a recent common ancestor. Several separate plant lineages, all under the same stress (nutrient-poor soil — low nitrogen and phosphorus), independently evolved carnivory.

Each lineage emphasizes different adaptations — mechanosensing (a more sensitive trigger), luring prey, digesting it with enzymes, and assimilating the nutrients — but they converge on the same overall strategy. (Real research: carnivory has independently evolved in at least 14 families of flowering plants.)

Proving a plant is carnivorous

A clean, real example of the scientific method — and a nice tie-in to convergent evolution.

Scientific question
→
Hypothesis (testable explanation)

In 1875, Darwin studied alpine Saxifraga plants with sticky hairs and suspected they might be carnivorous — but couldn't prove it. 150 years later, researchers studying Saxifraga candelabrum (grows in nutrient-poor rock crevices on the Tibetan Plateau, covered in sticky glandular hairs) put Darwin's hypothesis to the test.

To count as carnivorous, a plant must be shown to trap, digest, and absorb its prey

1

Trap

Mesh and glass enclosures showed the plant's floral scent lures insects in — but its sticky hairs selectively caught only small insects, sparing the large pollinators that keep the plant's own reproduction going.

2

Digest

A chemical that fluoresces green in the presence of digestive enzymes was applied to the hairs — they glowed, confirming active digestion was happening.

3

Absorb

Flies were labeled with a nitrogen isotope and fed to the plant. Two weeks later, that same isotope turned up in the plant's own tissue — direct proof it absorbed nutrients from its prey.

This confirmed carnivory in a brand-new plant family (Saxifragaceae) — and hints that carnivory could be hiding in many more ordinary-looking sticky-leaved plants than scientists currently know about.
